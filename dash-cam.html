Dash Cam Buying Guide: Buy for the Day You Actually Need the Footage

A dash cam is insurance you install yourself: 99% of its life is silent loops nobody watches, and its entire value is the one clip where a plate is readable and a light is provably green. That flips the usual electronics logic — you're not buying features, you're buying evidence quality under bad conditions: night, glare, heat, and a moment you didn't choose. Resolution: the plate-reading test

Forget cinematic footage — the question is whether a paused frame reads a license plate at distance, at night, in rain. 1080p fails that test more often than buyers expect; motion blur and compression smear exactly the details that matter. 1440p (2K) is the practical floor; 4K buys real margin for cropping and distance. Two specs modify the pixel math: the sensor (larger night-oriented sensors beat pixel count after dark — look for night-sample screenshots in reviews, not brand claims) and HDR/WDR, which stops headlights and tunnel exits from blowing out the frame. A 4K cam with a weak sensor loses to a good 1440p at night; this category rewards reading reviews with actual footage stills.

One camera or three: channels

Front-only covers the majority of disputes — the car that cut across, the light that was green. Front + rear (dual channel) covers the tailgater who becomes a rear-end claim, which is both one of the most common crash types and the classic he-said-she-said; the price bump is modest, the install (a cable snaked to the rear glass) is the real cost. Interior-facing third channels exist for rideshare and delivery drivers, where the cabin is the workplace — if that's you, it's not optional equipment. For most commuters: dual channel is the sweet spot, front-only is a respectable budget call.

Parking mode: the feature that needs wiring to be real

The box says "parking mode"; the fine print says it needs constant power. Plugged into a cigarette-lighter socket, most cams die with the ignition — the parking protection you paid for never runs. The real implementations: a hardwire kit (~$20 plus 30 minutes at the fuse box, with a low-voltage cutoff that protects the car battery) or a dedicated battery pack (pricier, kinder to the car battery, faster to install). Buffered parking mode — which saves the seconds before an impact — is the version worth having; motion-only versions mostly record parking-lot pigeons. If you park on the street, this feature set is arguably worth more than the fourth K of resolution.

Heat, cards, and the maintenance nobody mentions

Supercapacitors over batteries: windshields exceed 150°F in summer; lithium cells swell and die there, supercapacitors shrug. In any hot state this is a hard filter, and honest brands list it. High-endurance microSD only: dash cams rewrite continuously — ordinary cards die in months, and a dead card records nothing while the cam looks fine. Buy 128–256GB high-endurance, format in-camera monthly, and replace every couple of years; treat cards like brake pads, not like furniture. Mount choice: adhesive mounts survive heat better than suction; either way, mount behind the mirror, run the cable under the headliner (ten patient minutes with the included tool), and check your state's windshield-obstruction rules — some require corner placement. Power runs off the same 12V socket ecosystem as the phone mount; a dual-port adapter keeps both alive.

After the incident: make the footage count

The clip only helps if it survives. Most cams auto-lock impact clips (G-sensor), but belt-and-suspenders: hit the manual save button, then pull the footage off the card the same day — loop recording will eventually eat even locked files on a full card. Note that footage helps you with insurers and police reports; hand it over on your terms (export a copy, keep the original). And check the timestamp is right after daylight-saving changes — a wrong clock is the cheapest way to weaken your own evidence. GPS-stamped models sidestep most timestamp disputes entirely.

FAQ

What resolution reads plates?

1440p minimum, 4K for margin. Night performance depends on the sensor as much as pixels — judge by review footage.

Is a rear camera worth it?

Usually yes — rear-end disputes are the classic dash cam save, and the price bump is small.

Does parking mode work out of the box?

No — it needs a hardwire kit or battery pack. Socket power dies with the ignition.

Why did mine stop recording?

Probably the card. Use high-endurance microSD, format monthly, replace every couple of years.

Battery or supercapacitor?

Supercapacitor, always, anywhere summers are real.
