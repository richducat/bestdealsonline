Power Bank Buying Guide: The mAh Number Is Lying to You (a Little)

Power banks are sold on one number — milliamp-hours — and it's the least honest spec in consumer electronics. Not because brands lie about the cells, but because physics taxes every transfer: you'll pocket roughly 60–70% of the rated capacity as actual phone charge. Once you know that, sizing is easy, and the real differences move to wattage, weight, and safety. Capacity math you can do in your head

Take the rated mAh, multiply by 0.65, divide by your phone's battery (most phones: 4,000–5,000mAh). So:

  • 5,000mAh — about ⅔ of a phone charge. Pocket insurance, not a travel plan. The right size for "my phone dies at 9pm sometimes."
  • 10,000mAh — 1.5–2 phone charges. The commuter default: enough for a long day plus a top-up for someone else, still light enough to forget it's in the bag.
  • 20,000mAh — 3–4 phone charges or one small-laptop refill. Weekend trips, festivals, flights with broken seat power. Noticeably heavy — this is a bag item, not a pocket item.
  • 25,000–27,000mAh — the practical ceiling for flying (see airline rules below). Multi-day camping, work crews, hurricane kits — pair it with our power-station sizing guide for the bigger stuff.

Wattage: the spec that decides what it can charge

Capacity says how much energy the bank holds; output wattage says what it can usefully feed.

  • 10–12W (basic USB-A): slow phone charging. Fine overnight, frustrating at a layover.
  • 20–30W (USB-C Power Delivery): fast-charges every current phone and most tablets. This is the floor worth buying in 2026.
  • 45–65W: charges ultrabooks and MacBook Airs at usable speed. The bank needs the capacity to back it up — a 10,000mAh/65W bank empties into a laptop in under an hour.
  • 100W+: full-size laptop territory, priced accordingly. Check that sustained wattage matches the headline, not just a 30-second burst.

Two ports beat one if you'll ever share; check whether wattage splits when both are active (a "65W" bank often becomes 45W + 20W). And the cable matters — a cheap cable silently caps a 65W bank at 20W. Our USB-C cable guide covers the ratings printed on the connector.

Airline rules, in plain English

Power banks fly carry-on only — lithium cells are banned from checked bags, and gate agents do pull them. The limit that matters is watt-hours: up to 100Wh (≈27,000mAh) flies freely; 100–160Wh needs advance airline approval; over 160Wh stays home. The Wh figure is printed on the bank's casing — if a listing doesn't show that print clearly in photos, that's a quality tell in itself. International budget carriers are stricter about loose batteries in general, so keep the bank where you can produce it.

Safety and longevity: what actually kills them

The cells inside are commodity; the power-management board is where cheap banks cut corners. Signals worth paying for: a recognizable brand with a stated warranty, over-current/over-temperature protection listed as a spec rather than a vibe, and UL 2056 or equivalent certification. Habits that matter: don't store it in a hot car (the number one cell killer), store around half charge if it'll sit for months, and retire any bank that swells, whistles, or gets hot while idle — immediately, outdoors, not in the kitchen trash. Expect 300–500 full cycles from quality cells; that's 3–5 years of normal use.

Use-case shortcuts

Students: a 10,000mAh/20W bank covers a full campus day and lives in any backpack pocket — it's one of the few genuinely universal dorm gifts. Commuters: same size, but pay for the charge-percentage display; guessing is how you leave home with an empty bank. Travelers: 20,000mAh, 45W+, and check the Wh print for security lines. Emergency kit: buy two mid-size banks rather than one giant — redundancy beats capacity when one fails — and recharge them every six months when the clocks change. Magnetic (MagSafe-style) banks: convenient for phones that support them, but wireless transfer wastes ~30% of an already-taxed capacity; treat the mAh number as even softer, and see the wireless charger guide for the alignment rules that apply here too.

Reading a listing in 30 seconds

Four checks separate honest banks from junk before you ever open reviews. First, the Wh print: legitimate banks photograph the casing with capacity in both mAh and Wh; listings that hide the hardware behind renders usually have something to hide. Second, capacity vs. price sanity: quality cells cost real money, so a "30,000mAh" bank priced like a 10,000 is telling you the number is fiction. Third, wattage phrasing — "22.5W max" across three ports is not 22.5W per port; look for per-port figures. Fourth, scan the one-star reviews for the words swollen, hot, or stopped holding — those three words in quantity outweigh any star average. Thirty seconds, and you've filtered out most of the category's regret.

FAQ

How many charges from 10,000mAh?

1.5–2 full phone charges after conversion losses. Rated mAh × 0.65 ÷ your battery size is the honest math.

Can it fly with me?

Carry-on only. Under 100Wh (≈27,000mAh): no approval needed. The Wh number is printed on the casing.

What wattage for a laptop?

60W minimum for ultrabooks, 100W for bigger machines, always via USB-C Power Delivery.

Why did mine die young?

Heat and empty storage. No hot cars, store at ~50%, and quality cells last 300–500 cycles.

Pass-through charging okay?

Only when explicitly supported. Otherwise it cooks the cells.
