Wattage: buy for the hungriest device you own

USB-C Power Delivery is a negotiation: your device asks for what it wants, the charger provides it or its best lower offer. That has two practical consequences. First, overbuying is safe — a 100W brick feeds a phone exactly the 25W it requests. Second, underbuying is silent — a laptop on a 30W brick charges slowly or drains-while-charging without ever telling you why.

  • 20–30W: phones and earbuds at full speed. The single-device minimum worth owning in 2026.
  • 45W: tablets, small laptops in a pinch, and every phone made.
  • 65–70W: the sweet spot — ultrabooks, MacBook Airs, and every smaller device, in one brick.
  • 100–140W: full-size laptops and multi-device desks. Above 100W, confirm your cable is rated to carry it (see below).

Match the brick to the hungriest device you own, and everything smaller inherits full speed for free.

GaN: why the good bricks got tiny

Gallium-nitride circuitry switches faster and sheds less heat than the silicon in older chargers, which is why a modern 65W GaN brick is smaller than the 5W cube your phone shipped with a decade ago. This isn't a luxury tier anymore — GaN is simply how good chargers are built above ~30W. What it buys you in practice: half the bag weight, cooler operation (heat is what ages electronics on both ends of the cable), and folding-prong designs that survive a backpack. If a 65W charger is suspiciously large and cheap, it's old silicon design; that's not dangerous, just obsolete.

Multi-port math: the headline number splits

The trap in multi-port chargers is that the big number on the box is a total. A "65W" two-port brick usually delivers 45W + 20W with both ports busy — fine for laptop + phone, wrong for two laptops. Before buying, find the port-split table in the listing (honest brands publish it; its absence is a signal). Rules of thumb: laptop + phone wants 65W total; laptop + phone + earbuds wants 100W; a family charging station wants 100W+ with four ports. And one port should be USB-C on every brick you buy from here on — USB-A is legacy furniture now.

The cable is part of the charger

Above 60W, USB-C cables need a 5A rating with an e-marker chip to carry full power — a random drawer cable silently caps a 100W brick at 60W. Symptoms are invisible: no error, just slower charging. When you buy the brick, buy or verify the cable's printed rating with it. Safety: the only real risk in this category

Certified chargers from known brands are boring and safe; the fires come from uncertified no-names whose over-voltage and thermal protections are stickers rather than circuits. What to check: UL, ETL, or FCC listing stated as a certification (not "designed to meet"), a brand that publishes a warranty and support address, and reviews without the words melted, burning smell, or sparked. The savings between a certified 65W brick and a sketchy one is a few dollars; the downside is asymmetric. This is the one aisle where "cheapest listing" is genuinely bad advice.

Travel, dorm, and desk notes

Travel: one 65–100W GaN brick with folding prongs replaces every device's charger; GaN bricks are dual-voltage (100–240V) by design, so international trips need only a plug adapter, not a converter. Dorms: outlets are scarce currency — a single 4-port 100W station claims one outlet and charges a laptop, phone, earbuds, and a power bank simultaneously; it's the quiet hero of our dorm essentials list. Desks: mount the brick behind the monitor, run one rated cable to the front, and pair with a wireless stand for the phone — the drawer never refills.

FAQ

What wattage do I need?

Phone: 20–30W. Tablet: 45W. Ultrabook: 65W. Big laptop: 100W. Higher is always safe — devices take only what they ask for.

Is GaN worth it?

It's the modern default above 30W: same power, half the size, cooler running. Not a premium — just current technology.

Will 100W hurt my phone?

No. Power Delivery negotiates down to what the phone requests. The risk is uncertified junk, not honest wattage.

Why do two devices slow it down?

Total wattage splits across ports. Check the listing's split table, not the headline.

Does the cable matter?

Above 60W, yes — it needs a 5A/e-marker rating or it silently caps your speed.
