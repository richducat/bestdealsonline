Weighted Blanket Buying Guide: Weight, Size, Fill and Washability

A weighted blanket is one purchase where the sizing rules are the whole game: buy the right weight and size and it feels like a firm, calming hug; buy wrong and it's an expensive, sweaty tarp that slides off the bed. The good news is the rules are simple, and they have nothing to do with brand.

The 10% rule, and when to bend it

The standard starting point is a blanket at roughly 10% of your body weight: about 15 lb for a 150 lb adult, 20 lb for 200 lb. Within a couple of pounds either way is fine — the practical difference between 15 and 17 lb is small.

Bend the rule down if you're a first-timer who runs claustrophobic, you move a lot in your sleep, or you're between sizes. A blanket that's slightly too light still works; one that's clearly too heavy gets kicked to the floor by week two, which is the most common way these purchases fail. Couples sharing one blanket should ignore combined-weight math — a shared blanket sized off two bodies is too heavy for whichever person ends up under most of it. Two individual blankets genuinely work better.

Buy body-size, not bed-size

The most common sizing mistake is buying a queen blanket for a queen bed. A weighted blanket should cover you: a throw/twin size around 48"×72" is right for one adult. Anything that hangs over the mattress edge has its weight constantly pulling downward, and the blanket migrates to the floor overnight. Bed-size weighted blankets only make sense as a shared duvet replacement — and see above for why sharing usually disappoints.

Fill and construction: where quality actually lives

  • Glass microbeads are the fill to prefer: denser, so the blanket is thinner and drapes better, and quieter when you shift. Plastic pellets are cheaper, bulkier for the same weight, and can rustle.
  • Small quilted pockets (4–6" squares) keep the fill spread evenly. Big pockets let beads pool at one end — the classic cheap-blanket failure. Look closely at the stitching grid in photos.
  • Reinforced edge stitching matters more than on a normal blanket, because every night the fill presses outward against the seams.

Hot sleepers: this is the dealbreaker to solve first

Weight means layers, and layers mean warmth. If you already sleep hot, pick fabric before anything else: bamboo-derived viscose or cooling-weave cotton covers sleep meaningfully cooler than fleece, minky, or sherpa — those plush covers are lovely in January and unbearable in July. Some blankets sell a two-sided cover (plush one side, cool the other), which is the practical year-round answer. A knitted open-weave weighted blanket (no beads, just dense yarn) is the coolest-sleeping option of all, at the cost of a bulkier look.

Washing: check this before you buy

A 15–20 lb blanket does not go in a standard home washer without protest, and repeated washing is hard on fill pockets. Strongly prefer a removable, machine-washable cover with a tie or zip system that keeps the inner blanket in place — you wash the cover weekly like a duvet cover and the weighted insert rarely. If a blanket has no removable cover and the care label says spot-clean only, know what you're signing up for.

Safety, stated plainly

Weighted blankets are never for infants or toddlers, and not for anyone — child or adult — who cannot easily lift the blanket off themselves. For older kids, the same ~10% guidance applies once they can move freely under it; if there's any medical question (breathing, circulation, mobility), ask a doctor first. This is the one product category where "size up for the deal" is the wrong instinct every time.

What each price band actually buys

Under $50 gets a solid glass-bead blanket in common sizes, usually without a cover — fine as a first blanket. $50–100 typically adds the removable cover, better stitching, and cooling-fabric options; this is where most people should land. Above $100 is knitted designs, premium bamboo covers, and larger sizes — pay it for the fabric and construction, not for the weight itself, which costs the same in any blanket.

FAQ

How heavy should a weighted blanket be?

The common starting point is about 10% of your body weight, give or take a pound or two — a 15 lb blanket for someone around 150 lb. First-time users who are between sizes usually do better rounding down; too heavy feels restrictive rather than calming.

Should a weighted blanket be the same size as my bed?

No — it should match your body, not the mattress. A weighted blanket is a personal item: a twin/throw size (roughly 48 by 72 inches) covers one adult. Bed-width blankets hang over the edges, and the weight of the overhang drags the blanket off you overnight.

Are weighted blankets safe for kids?

Never use one for a baby or toddler, and don't use them with anyone who can't lift the blanket off themselves. For older children, common guidance is roughly 10% of the child's body weight and only once they can easily move it — when in doubt, ask your pediatrician.
